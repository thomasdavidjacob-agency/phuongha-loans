Complete Guide

Down payment assistance in Oregon & Washington.

By Phuong Ha · NMLS #1839449 · Licensed in Oregon & Washington

Let's be honest — who has an extra twenty percent sitting there? Ten? Even five? For most people the down payment is the single thing standing between renting and owning, and the most common reason buyers never make the call is a belief that they need a pile of cash they don't have.

They often don't. This is the full picture of how down payment assistance works in Oregon and Washington — what it is, who qualifies, which programs exist, and what to watch for. Every section links to a deeper article if you want to go further.

What down payment assistance actually is

DPA is help — money toward your down payment, and frequently your closing costs — so you don't have to bring the whole amount in cash. It generally comes in two forms:

  • Grants — money that doesn't have to be repaid, as long as you meet the program's conditions.
  • Second loans — a small additional loan alongside your main mortgage. Some are forgivable over time, some are deferred (repaid later, usually when you sell or refinance), and some carry a low monthly payment.

Most of these programs run through state housing finance agencies — Oregon Housing and Community Services and the Washington State Housing Finance Commission — alongside local county programs and lender-specific offerings. Each has its own rules, funding, and availability, and they do change.

Who tends to qualify

Every program differs, but the recurring themes are:

  • Income within program limits for your county and household size — usually higher than buyers assume.
  • A credit profile meeting the program minimum — often lower than buyers assume.
  • A primary residence — somewhere you'll actually live, not an investment property.
  • Sometimes a homebuyer education course — a short class, and a genuinely useful one.

First-time buyers are a common fit, but "first-time" is usually defined as not having owned a home in the past three years. That single detail qualifies a lot of people who'd written themselves off.

Programs in Oregon

Oregon's long-running Bond Residential Loan Program is being phased out and replaced by Flex Lending, which has two products. FirstHome is for first-time buyers and carries assistance currently set at 4% or 5% of the loan amount. NextStep has no first-time buyer requirement and caps household income at $125,000 — which makes it the one repeat buyers should ask about.

Both still pair a below-market interest rate with help toward your cash to close. One correction worth carrying with you: this assistance is structured as a loan that's repayable or forgivable, not as a grant. Forgivable is good, but it isn't the same as free.

Programs in Washington

The Washington State Housing Finance Commission runs several assistance programs. Home Advantage is the flagship, with House Key and Opportunity-area programs, an Energy Spark add-on for efficient homes, and additional options for veterans. If you're buying in Clark County and commuting into Portland, these are directly relevant to you.

Local help in the Portland–Vancouver metro

County-level assistance exists on top of the state programs, and it varies meaningfully between Multnomah, Washington, Clackamas, and Clark counties. Which side of a county line you buy on can change what's available to you — worth knowing before you narrow your home search.

If you're a teacher, first responder, or public employee

Teachers, firefighters, nurses, and other public employees may qualify for additional benefits, including the federal Good Neighbor Next Door program and various lender-specific offerings. If you serve your community for a living, check this before anything else.

How DPA works with your actual loan

Assistance isn't a loan type on its own — it sits alongside one. FHA pairs with DPA especially well, and it's the most common combination. Conventional loans can work with assistance under program-specific rules. VA and USDA already offer zero down for those who qualify, which sometimes makes assistance unnecessary and sometimes still leaves a role for it in covering closing costs.

It's not just the down payment

Closing costs surprise more buyers than the down payment does. Many assistance programs can be applied to closing costs too, and seller concessions are a separate lever that can cover more of them. In the right situation you can use both.

What happens later

The question I get most after "do I qualify" is what happens down the road. Briefly: a grant generally has nothing to repay. A deferred second loan is repaid when you sell or refinance. A forgivable loan shrinks over time and can disappear entirely if you stay put long enough. Some bond-backed programs carry a potential recapture tax — rarely triggered, but you should know it exists before you sign.

The myths worth clearing up

Five things people believe that keep them from help they'd qualify for: that DPA is only for people who are truly struggling; that sellers won't accept an offer using it; that you must be a first-time buyer; that it automatically raises your rate; and that there must be a catch. None of those hold up as stated.

How long it takes

A DPA-assisted purchase closes on a broadly normal timeline. There's one real difference in the process, and knowing about it up front is most of what keeps a deal on schedule — including what to tell a seller so your offer reads as strong as any other.
